MONEY TALK: CAREERS — ISSUE 04

Consultations, and how they work — fees and all

Why the meeting before the work is its own kind of work, and why professionals across every industry are right to charge for it.

Money Talk: Careers Fees, expertise & the professionals who charge for them 7 min read

Once again, I've found myself in a situation where a business approached me and tacitly sought to bypass paying consultation fees, all under the guise of asking for a "quotation." This article covers a few points which, hopefully, can answer the consultation question once and for all.

A consultation fee is a fee charged for the use of a professional's time, expertise, analysis, assessment, advice or initial problem-solving. It is not a formality before the "real" work begins — it is the real work of figuring out what the real work should be.

01 — The anatomy of a consultation
What actually happens in the room

A consultation, whatever the profession, tends to involve some or all of the following elements:

  • 1

    Physically or virtually meeting with a client

  • 2

    Understanding the client's requirements

  • 3

    Reviewing documents and other information

  • 4

    Diagnosing a problem

  • 5

    Providing professional advice

  • 6

    Developing preliminary recommendations and quotations

  • 7

    Strategising on the best way forward after the consultation

The fee is paid for the consultation itself — and it's common for a project to involve several consultations, depending on its scale.

A practical example

A client approaches an architect and says they want to build a house. Before preparing plans, the architect spends time discussing the client's requirements, examining the site's information and explaining possible design options. That initial professional advice is what the consultation fee pays for — not the finished plans.

02 — Two different questions
Consultation vs. quotation

The two are often mistaken for each other, or wrongly bundled together when they shouldn't be. Here's where they actually differ:

May be charged by the hour, session or fixed amountUsually represents a fixed price or pricing structure

03 — Why it matters
The significance of consultation fees

For the professional, the fee is a monetary indemnity for the time spent in a meeting with a prospective client — covering their time, experience and professional judgement.

There's no legally binding obligation on a client to proceed with the professional after the consultation, and the fee covers the bare minimum in the event the client declines, or can't afford, the quotation to proceed with the project.

Consultations sift clients seeking free advice from clients genuinely intending to pay. Many matters simply cannot be quoted without a necessary consultation first — one that builds a relationship between professional and client, and surfaces the critical information both parties need to reach a shared understanding of the matter and, eventually, a quotation. The process also lets the client calibrate their own expectations, and receive quality advice proportionate to the professional's experience.

04 — The other side
The disadvantages of consultation fees

Potential clients are often put off by having to pay for a consultation at all. Naturally, the fee tends to rise with the expertise sought, the professional's experience, and the duration of the session — usually billed per hour.

That fee can then become a stumbling block for a client who arrives with the misconception that they'll only ever pay for the actual project, i.e. the quotation.

05 — Across the professionsWhere consulting is the job before the job

From courtrooms to construction sites, the shape of a consultation changes — but the principle holds everywhere expertise is for hire.

Legal

A lawyer may charge for an initial consultation to understand a dispute, review relevant documents and explain possible legal options.

Consultation: R2,000 · work billed separately

Medical

A doctor's consultation is itself a professional service — taking a history, examination, diagnosis and treatment recommendations. Here, the consultation is often the primary service, not a preliminary step.

Accounting

An accountant might charge for an initial consultation with a business owner seeking advice on tax, bookkeeping or business structuring, leading to a separate proposal for ongoing services.

Engineering

An engineer might charge to inspect a structural problem before determining what remedial work is required — the assessment billed separately from the eventual repair.

Architecture

An architect could charge for an initial meeting and site assessment before developing detailed plans, establishing the client's requirements and the scope of work.

IT

An IT consultant might charge for diagnosing a network or cybersecurity problem before recommending a solution — the diagnosis leading to a separate implementation quote.

Marketing

A marketing consultant could charge for analysing a company's current strategy and providing recommendations, followed by a proposal for ongoing services.

Financial planning

A financial professional may charge for an initial consultation assessing a client's objectives and circumstances, before providing a detailed financial plan.

Automotive

A specialist mechanic may charge a diagnostic fee before determining repairs — a familiar case, since "how much to fix my car?" can rarely be answered responsibly upfront.

06 — ConclusionWhen consultation fees make sense

Not every consultation needs a fee. A short introductory conversation — brief, requiring little preparation, mainly there to check that client and professional are a good match — can reasonably be offered for free.

A fee becomes more appropriate once the client is seeking specific advice, documents need reviewing, research is required, or the professional must spend real time assessing a problem. At that point, the client is receiving expertise, not simply having a chat.

Professionals who charge should be transparent about what the fee covers: the duration, whether document review is included, what the client walks away with, and whether the fee is credited against future work. Clear communication prevents misunderstandings and lets the client decide with open eyes.

Clients, in turn, get more from a paid consultation by preparing questions in advance, bringing relevant documents, and stating the problem clearly — and by confirming upfront whether further work is billed separately, and whether the consultation fee comes off the eventual quote.

A consultation fee isn't simply a charge for a meeting. It's payment for a professional's time, knowledge, experience and judgement — and the one principle that never changes is transparency: both sides should know what's being paid for, what's being provided, and what happens next.
