The Sessions

Winning Culture

No script, no teleprompter. Just a guy and his thoughts — in the office, on the living room couch, or in the Sub Culture studio. This time: new gear, a neon billboard, and why most movies lose me inside twenty minutes.

The Sessions • 8 min read • Winning Culture

Morning, afternoon, evening — I don't know when you're listening. Thank you for your time, your ears, your thoughts and your feedback, written or spoken. For the first time in these sessions, I wrote my points down. If you want to understand the process, it's simple: one guy with his thoughts, sometimes in his office, sometimes on the couch, sometimes in the studio. I changed the workflow to make it more interesting, and I'm happy with where it landed. Which brings me to the gear.

01

New Toys

We've got a new camera, a new drone and a new 360 camera. I'm happy with the inventory, there's plenty more to add, and nobody can complain about what we managed to bring in this year.

Let's be clear about the drone, though. Drones are expensive, and an aerial shot is seasoning, not the meal. Keep it short, use it to establish the environment, maybe as a background theme. I wouldn't build a podcast around drone footage.

An aerial shot is seasoning, not the meal.

For the conversations, I'd rather do something else, and this is an announcement: do it live. Invite me to your church, mosque, synagogue, school, workplace, group or podcast. Bring me in and we'll talk. None of it is meant to be preachy. Take what you want and move on, because life happens and things change. Live is also where you'll see there's no script. These ideas get tattooed on my mind for a stretch, they get so overbearing I have to say them out loud, I drop them here, edit with the team, and I'm out.

I'm excited about adding video: guests, lighting, the whole art of storytelling. It's why I prefer theatre to movies. In film you can get away with a lot. Cut the scene, add VFX, add CGI. It's cool when it's done right, I'll admit that. But nothing beats watching someone do it live. Our talents differ. Some people can hold their breath longer, some process information faster, some tell better jokes, and some can tell you a story and keep you hooked until the last word. That's how I know someone's got it: I put my phone down and start rewinding, because I need to hear what was said, not just see the action.

Same goes for adaptations. When a show is based on a novel, I stay away from the book until I've finished the series. Reading first would wreck it. But we all know it's more fun to read the book and then learn it's being adapted, and that's the trap. Your imagination tells a better story than any camera can. Cameras are limited, directors see the story differently than you do, and studios cut whatever doesn't make money. So you sit there saying, "they got that wrong."

Readers never actually clash. Nobody fights over whether the character wears Adidas or Nike on a jog. We argue about why they killed that character. That's the beauty of it: leave some things to the imagination.

Sound is getting an upgrade too. I can't wait to do this with a live band, drums softly in the background, some guitar, maybe just a hymn. Not overdone. One of the reasons I've hated some church services, and I mean the delivery, not the message, is the band competing with the pastor. He raises his voice, so the drummer bangs harder, and it turns into a fucking mess. Some places do it beautifully. It all takes practice. Same in theatre, same live: know what you're saying, why you're saying it, and why it matters. If you don't say it, will anyone notice, or be offended that it wasn't said?

02

Time Has a Price

Once you work with that notion, you understand that time is valuable, and in the real world things cost. Not just money. Clients, too. Say you've got a budget and then you go on side missions. Was that necessary? How much did it cost? If we cut it, does the story suffer? Does the job suffer? Or was it just greed?

Work taught me this. I can't talk to you just to make you comfortable anymore. I give you my hourly rate, and once you know it, you appreciate the value of my time and get straight to the point. What would've taken five hours gets done in one, because nobody wants to pay me more than they can afford.

I learned it the hard way. In law you never know what's going on: abuse, a death, a dismissal. So I made myself available around the clock, out of genuine concern. People mistook that privilege for something else, and the people around you start developing theories of their own. State the rate. Set the parameters. Watch how fast the focus shows up.

03

The Neon Billboard

Speaking of work: the new billboard design is done, and it's beautiful. It's going up in the new space, I'm sending it to the designers, and it's going to be neon. I wanted the sound too, that electric hum. You'll catch it driving down Prince George Avenue, and from my second-floor office it's going to look gorgeous, especially at night.

I didn't want a billboard just because I can afford one. That defeats the purpose. It has to carry a message: advertise the law firm, advertise Sub Culture, and look good doing it. We played a lot with fonts and colours. You never know when someone's going to look at it, so I want it tattooed on your mind, so that when trouble finds you, ours is the first office you think of.

And aesthetics now go beyond the street. Not everyone has been to our office or lives in our area. But someone takes a photo: "Did you see that billboard? I didn't know there were lawyers in that building. I didn't know there was a studio in there." That's how it travels.

Next year is a big one. Fully booked, available only once or twice a month. That excites me, not just for me but for the jobs we've created and will create. It's simpler and more beautiful when you let others build themselves and earn their place. I'm not always going to be doing this. I want to leave a system that stays when I go: hard-working, transparent, cost-effective, honest.

I am not the system. I'm just one trying to make it better.

You'll see the billboard soon. We're still installing and sorting the cabling, and we'll post pictures, drone shots included.

04

Movies

I was watching a new South African movie on Netflix, and yes, you already know that's a warning sign. Let me keep this short before I truly offend. You don't need to sell your soul, and you definitely don't need sex scenes. I've covered this before, so briefly: between AI, VFX and CGI, there's plenty we can do without anyone compromising themselves. If it's artistic, you truly believe you have to, and it's compelling enough to earn recognition, money and staying power, then go for it. But it's not a necessity, and that's something I want to prove through Sub Culture. We're here to tell good, thought-provoking stories, not to put your relationships or your career at risk.

Filmmakers need a palette, and it takes more than one person to make a project work. Remember English Paper 3? One picture, write 500 words. The whole country looked at the same photo and came back with completely different stories. If it was a house, one kid wrote a family home where mum leaves for work. Another wrote a safe house with an informant inside, a backstory full of crimes, and a final act where the place isn't safe anymore and a gunfight breaks out. Someone gave it a tunnel: everyone thought the safe house was safe, and the whole time the guy was digging his way out. Same picture, different stories.

Same picture. Different stories.

You only notice the fingerprints once you see them. I watched Green Zone, Paul Greengrass and Matt Damon, the team behind The Bourne Supremacy, The Bourne Ultimatum and Jason Bourne. It's about Iraq, but swap the name tags and it's a Bourne movie in different clothes. The differences are subtle, yet you can tell it's a Greengrass film from how he shoots his angles, uses music and sound, and builds his colour palette. Michael Bay has fingerprints too, just glossier ones.

Which is why Joker: Folie à Deux hurt, especially if you loved the first one. It should've been the easy part. The cinematography was beautiful and the colours were still gorgeous. The music was overdone, but I understood it. The story just failed to get going. If a film doesn't earn enough of my attention for me to put the phone down, rewind, and work out who the main characters are and why they do what they do, it's over. I watched all of it anyway, because critics were panning it and I needed to see for myself. You keep rooting for something that keeps letting you down and going nowhere. It was a hard fucking watch, and I only finished it so I could be properly critical.

05

Why We Stay

Nobody has that patience anymore. The cinema is in your living room. If something offends you or misses your expectations, change the channel. Skip the track. Attention spans are short. If the TikTok is funny, cool. If it isn't, I'm not spending fourteen minutes decoding nonsense, especially after I've gone and bought snacks and popcorn.

Football's the same. If it's three-nil in the first twenty minutes, I'm going home. There's traffic, people are going to laugh in my face, and I've already wasted petrol, food, drinks and ticket money. If my team is embarrassing me, let me keep whatever reputation I have left, go home, drink it off, and not speak to anyone for a good minute. That isn't a lack of support. We've shown how supportive we can be.

The Kendrick Lamar event last year is the proof. Kendrick was on at 11 p.m., and the event started around noon. But there were things to do: a Ferris wheel, bars at different stages, local acts and smaller international acts to wander between. It rained, we were on a farm, it was muddy, and everyone still looked beautiful, amped for the day. You can see it on the Sub Culture YouTube page. Tickets were about five grand each, so ten grand for two, and that money was for Kendrick, because of who he is and what he's capable of. It was a must-see. Around six, when the main stage was running acts you didn't know, you had to feign your way through a bit.

Give people a reason to stay and they'll stand in the mud for hours. Give them nothing and they leave at three-nil.
