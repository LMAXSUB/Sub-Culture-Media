What Accountants Actually Earn in SA

From newly qualified CA(SA) to multi-million-rand CFO — the numbers behind the calculator, sourced, with their limits flagged.

Money Talk • 7 min read • Careers & Pay

Everyone assumes accountants are quietly rich. The truth is more interesting: a profession with a steep ladder, a very high ceiling, and salary data that is far messier than any spreadsheet would like you to believe.

We pulled figures from a recruitment benchmark, a salary aggregator and employer-reported government pay to map what accountants earn in South Africa and how the public sector compares. Every number below is attributed to its source in the list at the bottom, and where the data is thin or contradicts itself, we say so rather than smoothing it over.

The Ladder: Newly Qualified to CFO

This is the private-sector climb, from a newly qualified CA(SA) to Chief Financial Officer of a large corporate. Bars are scaled so the lower rungs stay visible instead of being flattened by the top. The jump between the first bar and the last is the whole story of the profession.

Newly Qualified CA(SA)R620K–R720K /yr
Finance Manager (non-CA)R650K–R900K /yr
Finance Manager (CA(SA))R900K–R1.35M /yr
Senior Finance ManagerR1.3M–R1.9M /yr
Head of FinanceR1.6M–R2.3M /yr
Finance Director (Large Corporate)R2.5M–R3.5M /yr
CFO (JSE / Large Corporate)R3.0M–R5.0M+ /yr

Source: Blue Recruiting, South Africa Finance Salary Benchmarks 2025/26. Bands are annual cost-to-company ranges from a corporate recruiter's placements; they vary by city, firm size and sector, so treat them as a map, not a guarantee. We have not charted pay during SAICA articles, because we could not verify a reliable published figure.

Blue Recruiting also notes that a CA(SA) with seven to ten years of post-qualifying experience earns more than double the starting package. The designation is the entry ticket, and experience is what moves you up the bars.

The government figures have no stated experience level and tiny samples; the private bands are cost-to-company from a recruiter's placements. These are different methods, so the gap is indicative, not exact.

So who pays more?

Honestly, the evidence is mixed. One aggregate says public wins narrowly, but it contradicts its own overall average. Role-by-role, government-reported pay runs well below private placement bands, but on very thin data. What no salary table captures is the rest of the package: pension, job security and predictable hours. If cash is your only metric, the private sector has the higher ceiling by a long way. If it isn't, government work is a defensible choice, and we'd be wary of anyone who tells you the numbers settle it.

The Bottom Line

Accounting isn't the overnight money story people assume it is — it's a long game with a genuinely brutal opening stretch. But the back half of that game, provided you push through to the CA(SA) designation and into the private sector's upper management, pays out better than almost any other career path available in South Africa without founding your own company. The spreadsheet, it turns out, eventually balances in your favour.
